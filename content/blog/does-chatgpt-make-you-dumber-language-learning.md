## Introduction

I asked myself a genuinely uncomfortable question recently: is using ChatGPT making me dumber? It came up while I was building my own website from scratch, coding it myself and leaning on ChatGPT constantly to troubleshoot errors along the way. At some point, I applied a fix it suggested without really thinking about whether it made sense — and it broke a large part of my site's configuration. That moment made me take the question seriously, and I think the answer matters just as much for language learners as it does for coders.

## The Familiar Fear

The image most people have in mind is a student asking ChatGPT to write an entire essay on French history for a school assignment, submitting it without engaging with the content at all. It's an easy scenario to picture because most of us have felt the pull of that shortcut at some point — the temptation to let the tool do the entire task rather than use it as an aid.

I experienced a version of this myself with my website. When I hit a coding problem I didn't fully understand, I'd sometimes just copy-paste ChatGPT's suggested fix directly into my code, without stopping to ask whether it actually addressed my specific issue. One of those blind copy-pastes ended up breaking a significant chunk of my site's settings — to the point where I genuinely wondered if I'd have to rebuild large parts of it from scratch.

## The Actual Difference: Whether You're Still Thinking

Reflecting on that experience alongside interviews with AI industry figures discussing this exact question, the answer that makes the most sense to me isn't about the tool itself — it's about whether you're still doing any thinking when you use it.

When I asked ChatGPT questions about problems I already partially understood — where I had a real opinion about what I needed, and just wanted help finding the specific fix — I actually learned something in the process. I came away understanding my own code better than before I asked. But when I asked vague, open-ended questions and simply applied whatever came back without evaluating it, I learned nothing, and in my case, actively created new problems by trusting an answer I hadn't verified.

The distinguishing factor isn't the AI's competence — it's whether you engage critically with what it gives you, or just accept it wholesale. Tools that push you to specify exactly what you need, to choose between options, to articulate your own reasoning before getting an answer, keep you actively thinking. Tools — or usage habits — that let you skip straight to "just do it for me" bypass that thinking entirely, and that's where the "getting dumber" concern actually has some substance.

## What This Means for Learning French

This distinction maps almost exactly onto how AI tools get used in language learning, and it's worth being deliberate about which mode you're in.

**Passive use, which risks real harm to your learning:**
- Asking ChatGPT to translate a sentence for you and copying the translation without trying it yourself first.
- Asking it to write an essay or a message in French for you to submit or send as your own work.
- Requesting a giant list of vocabulary and passively scrolling through it, expecting memorization to happen automatically.

**Active use, which genuinely helps:**
- Writing your own sentence in French first, then asking ChatGPT to correct it and explain *why* the correction is needed, not just what the correction is.
- Asking it to quiz you, or to choose between two options you propose, rather than simply generating a full answer from nothing.
- Using it to explain a specific grammar point you're already actively struggling with — where you have a genuine question, not just a gap you'd rather skip past.
- Practicing spoken or written conversation with it, where you're actively producing language and receiving targeted correction, rather than having it generate a monologue for you to read.

The common factor in every example on the "helps" side: you're still the one doing the primary linguistic work. The AI is correcting, explaining, and quizzing — not generating your understanding for you wholesale.

## A Practical Rule of Thumb

Before sending a prompt, it's worth pausing on a simple question: am I asking this because I want to understand something, or because I want to skip understanding it? If it's the former, you're very likely to walk away having learned something real, the same way I learned more about my own code when I asked focused, specific questions about problems I already partly grasped. If it's the latter, you'll get an answer — but you probably won't retain it, and depending on the stakes, you might create a bigger problem than the one you started with, the way I did with my website.

## Conclusion

AI doesn't make you dumber by existing — it makes you dumber if you let it replace your thinking instead of supporting it. For French learners specifically, that means using tools like ChatGPT to correct your own attempts, explain concepts you're actively wrestling with, and quiz you on material you're building — not to generate finished language for you to passively consume. The tool hasn't changed what it takes to actually learn a language; it's just made it easier than ever to quietly skip the parts that do the real work, if you're not paying attention to which mode you're in.
