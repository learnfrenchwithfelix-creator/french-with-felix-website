## Introduction

A lot of learners eventually ask themselves the same question: should I take an official exam to validate my level? I've asked myself the exact same thing about Japanese, where the equivalent test is the **JLPT** (Japanese Language Proficiency Test), the rough counterpart to the CEFR scale (A1 to C2) used for French with exams like the **DELF** and **DALF**. After passing JLPT N3 a few years ago — roughly equivalent to A2/B1 — and preparing (on and off) for N2, I've landed on an answer that's more nuanced than a simple yes or no.

## The Anecdote That Made Me Rethink It

Before leaving Japan, I bought three JLPT N2 prep books: a grammar book, a reading comprehension book, and a vocabulary and exercises book. Back in France, a Japanese friend visiting flipped through the grammar book and said, "Hmm, we never use this — this grammar isn't really used." That single comment made me question something I'd taken for granted: is what these exams test actually useful?

## Active Knowledge vs. Passive Knowledge

This is the key distinction. **Active knowledge** is what you can produce — words and structures you use when speaking or writing. **Passive knowledge** is what you can recognize and understand, even if you'd never generate it yourself. Studies suggest a native speaker actively uses somewhere around 5,000 to 10,000 words, while passively recognizing 30,000 to 40,000. We understand far more than we ever produce — in every language, including our own.

This gap explains why exams can feel disconnected from real fluency at higher levels. The **passé simple** (simple past tense) is the clearest example in French. You'll find it constantly in literature, but virtually never in spoken French — even native speakers would struggle to conjugate most verbs correctly in it on demand. It exists almost entirely as passive knowledge: something you recognize while reading, never something you actively produce.

## When Exams Are Genuinely Useful

At beginner and lower-intermediate levels, official tests are worth taking seriously. A1 through B1 content covers the theoretical backbone you actually need — être and avoir, present tense, futur proche, passé composé, imparfait. Without these, you can't understand large parts of the language or express yourself at all. I teach these tenses early and deliberately with every new student for exactly this reason: they're not optional, and testing them makes sense.

## Where It Gets Less Practical

As you climb toward C1/C2, exam content increasingly tests knowledge that's more academic than functional. The subjonctif présent is still genuinely used in everyday French ("il faut que je fasse ça"), so that one earns its place. But some grammar tested at advanced levels — like the passé simple — is something even highly educated native speakers rarely, if ever, use actively.

A more everyday (and honestly trickier) example is **past participle agreement**. With **être**, the participle always agrees with the subject: *il est venu* / *elle est venue* / *elles sont venues*. With **avoir**, it's more complicated — the participle only agrees if the direct object comes *before* the verb: *j'ai mangé des pommes* (no agreement) versus *les pommes que j'ai mangées* (agreement, because "les pommes" comes first). This rule trips up French people constantly in writing, precisely because it's a rule most of us apply passively at best, if we apply it at all.

## What Exams Are Actually Good For

Setting the "is it useful" question aside, exams have real secondary benefits:

- **They give you a concrete goal.** Motivation can wane at intermediate levels, and having a test date on the calendar is a strong reason to keep showing up daily.
- **They force variety.** Exam prep demands a broad vocabulary across subjects — politics, economy, entertainment, current events — which pushes you out of the narrow, repetitive content you might otherwise default to on your own.
- **They build real concentration.** Sitting with a text for two to four hours, focused entirely on the language in front of you, is a rare and valuable kind of deep focus in an age of constant notifications. There's something almost meditative about it.

## How to Decide

Ask yourself what you actually want out of your French: passive comprehension, or active fluency? If you just want to *recognize* the passé simple when reading a novel, you don't need to drill conjugating it — reading enough books will get you there naturally. If your goal is genuine spoken fluency, an exam focused heavily on formal, written grammar may not be the most efficient use of your study time.

That doesn't mean skip it. If a test date gives you the structure and motivation to keep learning consistently, that alone can be worth the (admittedly limited) practical value of the content itself.

## Conclusion

Official exams aren't useless, but they're not the whole picture either. Early on, they validate genuinely essential grammar. Later on, some of what they test drifts into passive, largely academic territory that even native speakers don't fully command. The right question isn't "is this exam useful?" — it's "do I need this specific motivation and structure right now?" If the answer is yes, sign up. If not, don't waste your time or money chasing a certificate you don't actually need.
