## Introduction

A language is really four skills: listening, speaking, reading, and writing. Reading tends to get less attention in immersion advice than listening or speaking, but skipping it cuts you off from a huge portion of both the language and its culture — you can technically speak a language fluently and still be unable to read it, which is a real and common gap, especially for languages with a difficult writing system. Reading is also one of the most efficient ways to expand your vocabulary, since written language draws on a noticeably larger pool of words than everyday speech — spoken French tends to use around 10,000 words in common use, while written French can range up to 30,000 or 40,000. Here are six things worth knowing before you start.

## Tip 1: Choose the Right Level

The idea of "learn French by reading novels" sounds appealing, but it's only half true — reading a native-level book demands an advanced level, arguably even more than watching native videos does, because you can't fall back on tone of voice or visuals to help you guess.

Here's a trap worth knowing about specifically: some books have a reputation for being "easy" that doesn't hold up. *Le Petit Prince* is the classic example — short, universally recommended to beginners, and genuinely much harder than its reputation suggests. It's written in a literary style full of *passé simple*, a past tense used almost exclusively in writing and virtually never in speech, with highly irregular verb forms (*être* becomes *je fus*, unrecognizable next to the verb's other forms). Books like this are better suited to advanced learners, not beginners looking for an easy first read.

A better starting category, in my experience, is personal-development or business books — the kind with a single core idea repeated through multiple examples. They're usually written almost entirely in *passé composé* rather than *passé simple*, tend to be shorter, and their repetitive structure is genuinely helpful for a learner, since the same vocabulary and structures keep resurfacing.

## Tip 2: Be Aware of Specific Vocabulary "Islands"

Books cluster around specific vocabulary depending on their subject — think of each topic as its own "island" of words. A book about work will bring you words like "manager," "responsibility," "merger." A biography of a footballer will bring you terms specific to sport: defender, goalkeeper, referee. If you open a book and suddenly feel like you know far less vocabulary than you thought, it's often not a sign you've regressed — it's just that you've landed on a new island you haven't explored yet.

This cuts both ways usefully: choosing books deliberately by subject is a good way to expand into vocabulary areas you might otherwise never encounter. If you're interested in sports, cinema, cooking, or any specific hobby, seek out books on that exact subject — you'll pick up a cluster of related, useful vocabulary all at once.

## Tip 3: Be a Detective

Your brain is a guessing machine, and reading is one of the best places to put that to work. E-readers with a built-in dictionary are genuinely convenient — tap a word, get the definition instantly — but that convenience is also a trap. The temptation to translate every single unfamiliar word removes exactly the skill you're trying to build.

Your brain learns better through guessing, anticipating, and then confirming than through being handed an answer directly. If you see a definition passively, without first trying to work it out, you're far more likely to forget it quickly. This applies at every stage — even native speakers guess constantly when reading something with a rich vocabulary, like a challenging author such as Marcel Proust. It's a skill everyone uses for life, in every language, including their own. Try to read a section fully before reaching for any translation, focusing on the general meaning first.

## Tip 4: Pause and Reread

Reading in a foreign language is genuinely tiring — it demands real mental effort, especially early on. It's completely normal to only get through a couple of pages before needing a break, the same way any new physical activity exhausts you at first and gets easier with repeated practice.

Don't be discouraged by slow progress in terms of pages per session. It's also worth deliberately rereading — going back over yesterday's pages before continuing helps reinforce what you half-remember, and rereading tends to go noticeably faster the second time, since you already have the gist. Progressing slowly through a 250-page book is completely fine; the goal is steady progress, not speed.

## Tip 5: Know Your Reading Style

There are, broadly, two different modes of reading, and it's worth being honest with yourself about which one you're in. There's **pleasure reading** — relaxed, low-pressure, moving past anything you don't fully understand without dwelling on it — and there's **"tryhard" reading**, where you're deliberately working to understand everything precisely, because you're using the book specifically to train your language skills.

Neither is wrong; they serve different purposes. If a particular book, in a particular moment, is meant to be enjoyable and relaxing, don't be hard on yourself about skipping unclear passages. If you've deliberately picked something to work on specific grammar or vocabulary, then it's worth slowing down and staying genuinely focused. The mistake is not being clear with yourself about which mode you're in — that's what leads to frustration, either from working too hard at something meant to be relaxing, or from not getting enough out of something meant to be a real study session.

## Tip 6: Give Yourself a Specific Focus

When I was working specifically on Portuguese conjugation, I brought that focus directly into my reading — deliberately paying attention to which tense each verb was in, checking whether it was past, imperfect, or something else, as I read. This turns reading into a way of reinforcing whatever you're separately studying, whether that's a grammar point from a course or something you noticed on your own.

If you're working on a specific structure, try to spot it in whatever you're reading — it reinforces the theory through real examples, and connects abstract grammar rules to language you're actually encountering. How deliberately you do this depends on your reading style from Tip 5, but it's a useful habit to have available whenever you want it.

## Conclusion

Reading is one of the richest ways to build real vocabulary and deepen your connection to French, but it comes with its own specific pitfalls — choosing a falsely "easy" book, over-translating instead of guessing, or not being honest with yourself about whether you're reading for pleasure or for practice. Get those right, and reading stops being a chore you feel obligated to squeeze in, and becomes one of the most naturally rewarding parts of learning the language.
