## Introduction

When I decided to leave my previous career to become a French teacher and content creator, I asked myself a question that probably occurs to anyone starting a career right now: is AI going to make this obsolete before I even get going? It's a fair question, and one I've thought about seriously rather than dismissing outright — I want to share where I've genuinely landed on it.

## Will People Still Bother Learning Languages at All?

Before getting to teachers specifically, there's a more basic question: with automatic translation improving constantly — from Google Translate's camera feature to the promise of real-time AI interpretation — will people still want to learn languages at all?

I think yes, even if the overall number of learners declines somewhat. Language learning opens doors to a culture in a way translation never fully replicates, and it's genuinely good exercise for memory and cognitive flexibility. There will always be people drawn to it for reasons that have nothing to do with practical necessity — the same way people still run marathons despite the invention of cars, or climb cliffs despite elevators existing. Not every human activity is chosen purely for efficiency.

## Has AI Really Revolutionized Access to Knowledge?

Here's a distinction I think gets missed in a lot of AI discourse: the internet already revolutionized access to knowledge, long before ChatGPT existed. Harvard courses, complete language curricula, endless free resources — all of that was already available online, for free, before any large language model existed.

What AI actually changed is different: not *access* to knowledge, but the *ease of consuming* it. Previously, finding the right information for a specific question meant searching multiple sites and synthesizing scattered results yourself. ChatGPT does that synthesis instantly. That's a real, significant change — but it's a change in convenience, not in whether the knowledge was reachable at all.

On pure information delivery, I think ChatGPT genuinely does compete with a language teacher. If a student needs to know how a grammar rule works, an AI can explain it clearly, quickly, and for free.

## What a Teacher Still Does That AI Doesn't

Where I think the comparison breaks down is **pedagogy** — not knowledge itself, but the craft of adapting how that knowledge gets delivered to a specific person. Some of my students want pure conversation practice, learning almost incidentally through talking. Others need to see explicit grammar rules laid out before anything clicks. Recognizing which kind of learner is in front of you, and adjusting on the fly, is a skill built from experience with real, different people — not from having access to information.

I also think there's real value in having a teacher who has personally struggled with language learning as a student, not just as an instructor. I currently study Japanese, Portuguese, and a bit of Korean, which means I feel the specific frustrations my own students feel, even though French itself came to me naturally as a native speaker. When a Japanese student struggles to pronounce the French "r," or when I struggled with Mandarin pronunciation myself, that shared experience of genuine difficulty is something I don't think an AI model reproduces — it can describe the difficulty, but it hasn't lived it.

Right now, ChatGPT's responses, however accurate, tend to stay somewhat flat and general. It doesn't sense when a student is quietly demoted or stuck in a way that needs addressing beyond the literal question asked. A good teacher notices that shift and responds to the person, not just the query.

## Content Creation: A More Uncertain Answer

On the content-creation side — the podcasts, videos, and articles I make — I'm honestly less certain, and somewhat more pessimistic. Fairly generic, mainstream content like a slow-French podcast talking about everyday topics isn't, technically, that hard to replicate with an AI prompt. I've already seen AI-generated language-learning content on TikTok and Instagram: robotic voices, generic slow French, functional but hollow.

The deeper question this raises extends beyond language learning — into music, film, and entertainment generally. Will there eventually be a genuinely good, entirely AI-generated film? I don't know. What I do know is that, personally, I'll keep seeking out music and films made by actual humans, because that's where I think the specific texture — the imperfection, the intentional choices, the emotional stakes of someone actually meaning something — comes from. Content generated purely by an algorithm optimizing for engagement carries a different, thinner kind of interest for me, even when it's technically competent.

For purely educational content, like slow, clear French for learners, I have less certainty. It's possible AI-generated versions of exactly this kind of content become genuinely good enough that the human behind it stops mattering to the audience. I don't have a confident prediction either way.

## Where I've Landed

Honestly, my answer isn't a clean, confident one. I go back and forth between optimism and pessimism depending on the day. What keeps me doing this work isn't a bet on AI's trajectory — it's that I genuinely enjoy teaching and making this content, and I believe it helps people. If I were doing this purely as an opportunistic bet on a growing market, I think I'd have already stopped, given how uncertain that bet looks right now.

## Conclusion

AI hasn't made language learning pointless, and it probably won't make human teachers pointless either — at least not for the parts of teaching that depend on adapting to a real, specific person's struggles. What's genuinely uncertain is the value of generic content creation, where AI is already closing the gap fast. I don't have a tidy answer to give you here, and I'd be skeptical of anyone who claims they do. What I can say is that as long as this work still feels useful and worth doing, I'll keep doing it — and I'd rather be honest about the uncertainty than pretend otherwise.
