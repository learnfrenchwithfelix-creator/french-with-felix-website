## Introduction

My name is Félix, and I'm a French teacher: I teach French to students all over the world. I became a teacher because I love learning languages. I've been learning Japanese for six years, and in that time I've tried a lot of methods.

In this article, I'll explain how I learn Japanese, how I teach French to my students, and the one distinction you need to understand to learn French well: the difference between **theoretical learning** and **natural learning**.

## Theory Comes First, and That's Normal

When you start learning a language, you tend to learn the theory: grammar rules, for example. That's normal. At the beginning, you need to understand how the language works.

Take sentence structure. French is an **SVO** language: subject, verb, object.

> <span lang="fr">*Je mange une pomme.*</span> (I eat an apple.)

<span lang="fr">*Je*</span> is the subject, <span lang="fr">*mange*</span> is the verb, <span lang="fr">*une pomme*</span> is the object. Every French sentence follows this structure.

That may seem obvious to you, but other structures exist. Japanese, for example, is an **SOV** language: subject, object, verb. In Japanese, you don't say "I eat an apple," you say "I an apple eat": *watashi wa ringo taberu*.

So at the start, you learn the theory to understand how the language works. But there's a trap.

## The Trap: Understanding a Rule Isn't Mastering It

The trap is thinking that if you know all the theory, all the grammar rules, you'll be able to understand French and to speak it.

Learning theory is satisfying, because it's a list of rules to learn, a bit like a to-do list: "OK, I've learned the present. OK, I've learned the passé composé. OK, I've learned the imparfait."

But the reality is that **you can understand a rule without mastering it**.

## The Cooking Analogy

Think about learning to cook. There are two things to know:

- **the tools**: a frying pan, an oven, and so on;
- **the ingredients**: tomatoes, onions, garlic, and so on.

Which do you think matters most: knowing every tool perfectly, or knowing as many ingredients as possible?

For me, it's the ingredients. You can cook with a single tool, but if you know lots of ingredients, you can make lots of different dishes. If you can use a frying pan and an oven perfectly but the only ingredient you know is potatoes, you won't be able to make much.

Learning a language works the same way. If you know lots of grammar rules but not much vocabulary, it won't get you anywhere. You can know every conjugation, but without words to conjugate, they're useless. (I explain this in more detail in [why vocabulary matters more than grammar](/blog/french-vocabulary-more-important-than-grammar/).)

So purely theoretical learning has two problems:

1. you can learn a rule without mastering it;
2. without vocabulary, all the rules in the world won't help you.

## The Solution: Immersion

What I do to learn Japanese, and the way I teach French, is **immersion**: watching a video in French, reading a text, listening to a podcast. In other words, *comprehensible input*. It really is the key to learning a language.

When you do immersion, you see all the theoretical rules **in action**. Your brain needs to see rules in action to absorb them, to digest the information. You have to feed your brain: it digests, and then you master the rules. (If you're curious about how that works, I wrote about [how your brain actually learns French](/blog/how-your-brain-learns-french-patterns/).)

I can explain how the passé composé works. But if you never see the passé composé in action, you won't master it.

## My Recommendation: A Quick Explanation, Then Immersion

So here's what I recommend:

1. **Look at the theoretical explanation**, without going into too much detail. Just a quick look.
2. **Then do immersion**: read a text, listen to a podcast, watch a video.

Through immersion, the rule becomes more natural, and you'll get a more natural command of grammar.

At the same time, comprehensible input teaches you **vocabulary**. When you watch videos, you see the same words several times in different sentences, for example <span lang="fr">*je peux faire quelque chose*</span>, <span lang="fr">*je peux aller quelque part*</span>. That repetition helps you absorb new words.

For me, that's the ideal mix: you learn the theory and you learn vocabulary.

## How I Teach It

That's exactly how I run my French lessons. I explain a rule quickly, without too many details. Then we read a text, listen to audio, and look at the rule in action.

Sometimes, while reading, a student asks: "Why do we use the rule like this in this situation?" That's when we look at a bit more theory. But the rule in action always comes first, and the questions come from there.

For the passé composé, for example: a short video lesson that goes straight to example sentences using it, then a text and an audio to practice a little every day, using that same rule.

## Conclusion

My advice is simple: when you learn a grammar rule, **do immersion**. Watch the rule in action if you want to master it.

Theory is useful, but you learn a language through immersion: listening, watching videos, reading texts. Your progress in French will depend on **the time you spend consuming the language**. So try to consume as much content at your level as you can. (And don't worry if you don't understand everything: [you don't need to](/blog/you-dont-need-to-understand-everything/).)
