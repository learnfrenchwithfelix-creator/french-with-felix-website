Here's a question I find genuinely fascinating — and slightly uncomfortable to answer, because I'm a French teacher who learns languages entirely on his own.

I'm currently learning Japanese and Portuguese without a tutor. No classes, no teacher. Just immersion, free resources, and consistency. And I'm convinced that in today's world, you can learn *any* language for free if you're willing to put in the work.

So the question becomes obvious: **what's the point of a French tutor at all?**

I've thought about this a lot. And after years of teaching French online, I have an honest answer — one that might surprise you.

---

## What Does a French Tutor Actually Do?

Before we talk about whether a tutor is worth it, let's be clear about what a tutor's job actually is.

### 1. Help You Reach Your Goals

This sounds obvious, but it's worth unpacking. Language learning has several distinct skills: listening, reading, speaking, and writing. Think of them like stats in a video game — you're always trying to level them up, ideally all at once, but sometimes you need to focus on specific ones.

The classic example is *"I understand French, but I can't speak it."* That's a very common situation where your listening stat is high but your speaking stat lags behind. A tutor's job is to identify this imbalance and help you work on the right skill at the right time.

### 2. Have Linguistic Knowledge You Don't

Here's something most people don't think about: native speakers don't actually know the rules of their own language.

I'm French. I speak French perfectly. But sometimes a student asks me *"why do you say it like that?"* and I need a few seconds to think. I know it sounds right — I just don't always know *why*. Because I learned it naturally, without studying the rules.

A good tutor has linguistic knowledge on top of native fluency. They can explain *why* you say *"il faut que j'aille"* (it's the subjunctive), why you use *être* instead of *avoir* in certain compound tenses, and which grammar rules actually matter for real conversation versus which ones only show up in textbooks.

### 3. Evaluate and Prioritize

Not all grammar is equally important. French grammar theory is technically infinite — there's always something more obscure to learn. But a good tutor knows what to prioritize.

A perfect example: many French textbooks open with subject-verb inversion questions (*Avez-vous un stylo?*). In theory, this is grammatically correct. In practice, almost no French person uses this in daily conversation. We say *"est-ce que vous avez un stylo?"* or simply *"vous avez un stylo?"* with rising intonation.

A tutor who understands how French is actually spoken will save you from wasting months on structures you'll never use in real life.

### 4. Adapt to Your Level in Real Time

This is, in my opinion, a tutor's most underrated skill — and the one that makes the biggest difference.

Let me share a personal story. When I lived in Japan with an intermediate Japanese level, I once had to open a bank account. The employee at the counter spoke at full native speed. When I didn't understand and asked her to slow down, she repeated the exact same sentence at the exact same speed. I understood nothing. I left feeling like my Japanese was terrible.

Contrast that with a Japanese farmer we stayed with during our time there. She had hosted dozens of foreign travelers and had naturally developed a pedagogical instinct. When we didn't understand something, she would slow down, rephrase, use simpler words — until we got it. Conversations flowed. We made progress.

That ability to adapt — to rephrase when you're stuck, to find another way to express an idea, to keep the conversation moving — is what separates a good tutor from just "talking to a native." It builds your confidence and makes the whole experience feel productive instead of frustrating.

## The Real Value of a Tutor: Speaking Practice

Here's my honest take: **for most of what you need to learn, you don't need a tutor.**

Vocabulary? Free resources everywhere. Grammar? YouTube, apps, blogs. Listening comprehension? Podcasts, series, films. Reading? Books, articles, news.

The one thing you genuinely struggle to practice alone is **speaking**.

And that's where a tutor's value is highest. When you book a session and someone is there for one hour, fully dedicated to making you speak — adapting to your level, correcting your errors in context, asking you questions about topics you actually care about — that's something you can't easily replicate on your own.

Yes, there are language exchange apps (HelloTalk, Tandem, etc.). But they come with a major limitation: it's hard to find a partner at exactly your level, and even harder to find someone with the teaching instincts to help you through the moments where you get stuck.

A tutor handles both problems automatically.

---

## The Main Downside: Cost

I'm going to be honest about this too, because I think it's important.

Tutors are expensive. And there's a real reason for it.

When you pay for a private lesson, you're paying for one person's full attention for one hour. That person also has preparation time, administrative work, taxes to pay, and a limit on how many hours they can teach before burning out. I personally cap my teaching hours at around 20–25 per week — any more and I'm too tired to do it well.

All of this means private tutoring will always cost more than apps, podcasts, or group classes. That's just the reality.

If cost is a factor for you, the question to ask yourself isn't *"can I afford a tutor?"* but *"how can I maximize the value of each session?"*

---

## The Trap Most Students Fall Into

Here's the most common mistake I see — and it's one I want you to avoid.

**Treating tutoring as your only French practice.**

I've seen students take one or two lessons per week, feel good about it, and make almost no progress after a year. Not because the lessons were bad — but because their brain was only exposed to French for two hours a week.

Your brain needs *volume*. It needs consistent, repeated exposure to the language before things start to stick automatically. Lessons accelerate the process, but they can't replace it.

The right approach looks like this: **immersion as your foundation, tutoring as your accelerator.**

Do your immersion work daily — podcasts, shows, reading, whatever you enjoy. Then come to your lesson having already spent time inside the language. You'll practice things you've already encountered, your brain will make connections faster, and you'll actually *feel* your progress during the session.

This is when tutoring becomes genuinely worth the investment: when it's layered on top of immersion, not used instead of it.

---

## A Bonus Reason to Work With a Tutor

One last thing that doesn't get talked about enough: **cultural exchange**.

When you work with a native French tutor, you're not just practicing the language — you're talking to someone who actually lives in France (or another French-speaking country), knows the culture from the inside, and can answer your real questions.

Why do French people go on strike so often? What do people actually talk about at dinner? Is it rude to ask someone their salary? 

These are things you can't Google your way to. But you can ask your tutor.

---

## So, Do You Need a French Tutor?

Here's my final answer:

**Yes — if you use them correctly.**

A tutor is most valuable for speaking practice, personalized feedback, and cultural insight. They're not a replacement for immersion — they're a complement to it.

If budget is a concern, don't spread yourself too thin across daily lessons you can't really afford. Instead, do one focused session per week, prepared and with a clear goal, while keeping up your immersion work in between. You'll get more out of that than four rushed, passive lessons.
