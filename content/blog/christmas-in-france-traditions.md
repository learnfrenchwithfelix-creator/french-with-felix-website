## Introduction

Christmas in France follows a shape that will feel familiar to most Western countries, but with enough specific traditions and small differences to be worth exploring — especially if you're learning French and want to understand the vocabulary and customs that come up every December. Here's how it typically unfolds, based on how my own family has always done it — not a universal rule, but a fairly representative picture.

## The Season Begins Right After Halloween

Christmas decorations start appearing in French towns almost the moment Halloween ends, on November 1st. String lights, garlands, and window displays go up across town centers, and shops shift into full Christmas mode — trees, Santa figures, the works. There's even a well-documented, slightly funny statistic showing Google searches for Mariah Carey's Christmas music spiking reliably every November — a fairly universal sign that the season has officially begun.

## Le Calendrier de l'Avent

One of the most beloved traditions, for children and adults alike, is the **calendrier de l'avent** (advent calendar) — a calendar with one small door or compartment for each day of December, usually containing a piece of chocolate, that runs until December 24th. It's generally seen as something for kids, but plenty of adults — myself included, at 31 — keep the tradition going. It's less about the chocolate and more about building anticipation day by day as Christmas approaches.

## Writing to Père Noël

Children in France traditionally write a letter to **Père Noël** (Father Christmas) listing what they'd like as gifts. The letter usually opens with something like "*Bonjour Père Noël, cette année j'ai été très sage*" ("Hello Father Christmas, I've been very good this year") before listing the requested toys. Parents traditionally tell kids the letters need to be sent to Père Noël's home in Lapland — though, naturally, it's the parents who quietly do the actual shopping afterward, based on what's realistic. Christmas catalogs full of toys used to be a common way for kids to circle or cut out what they wanted, to help build their list.

## Christmas Films

French television leans heavily into Christmas films throughout December — some genuinely built around the holiday, like *Love Actually*, and some that have simply become associated with the season through repeated yearly viewing, like *Harry Potter* or *The Lord of the Rings*. Watching the same familiar films once a year, right around Christmas, is part of the ritual for a lot of families, regardless of whether the film has anything to do with the holiday itself.

## Le Sapin de Noël

The Christmas tree (**le sapin de Noël**) is a near-universal fixture — either a real tree, widely sold in supermarkets during the season, or an artificial one reused year after year. Most families wait until at least December 1st to put it up and decorate it with garlands and ornaments, gradually building the festive atmosphere as the month goes on, rather than starting too early.

## Réveillon and Christmas Day

The main celebrations happen across two dates: **le réveillon de Noël**, the evening of December 24th, and Christmas Day itself, December 25th. Traditionally, families gather for a festive dinner on the 24th and another meal — usually lunch — on the 25th. It's worth knowing that in France, only December 25th is an official public holiday; the 24th is often still a working day, and anyone required to work on the 25th itself is legally entitled to higher pay, since it's a holiday.

## When Do the Presents Get Opened?

This is where French families genuinely differ. Some open presents on the night of the 24th, right after dinner — sometimes as late as midnight. Others, like my own family growing up, wait until Christmas morning on the 25th, which comes with its own particular magic: waking up to find the presents already sitting under the tree, seemingly appeared overnight.

That version of the tradition comes with a very specific kind of torture: lying in bed on the night of the 24th, desperately trying to fall asleep so morning — and the presents — would arrive faster, and failing completely. Add to that the shared excitement of sleeping in the same room as cousins at a grandparent's house, and Christmas Eve night becomes a fairly universal experience of near-insomnia for French kids.

It's also common, particularly in families who open presents on the 24th, for an adult to dress up as Père Noël to physically deliver the gifts — to the genuine, wide-eyed shock of small children, who tend to be simultaneously thrilled by the sack full of presents and a little afraid of the mysterious man carrying it.

## As Adults, the Traditions Soften

As an adult, the core rhythm stays the same — a meal on the 24th, another on the 25th — but the presents usually get handed out directly rather than through the Père Noël ritual, and some of the childhood magic naturally fades. What stays constant, regardless of age, is the emphasis on gathering the family together to eat and spend time with each other, which remains the actual heart of the holiday well after the belief in Père Noël disappears.

## Conclusion

A French Christmas shares its basic shape with most Western celebrations — a tree, gifts, a family meal — but the specific rituals around it, from the advent calendar to the timing of gift-opening to the superstition around who plays Père Noël, give it its own distinct texture. If you're learning French, December is a great time to notice this vocabulary showing up everywhere: in shop windows, on the news, and in conversation with anyone counting down the days until *le réveillon*.
