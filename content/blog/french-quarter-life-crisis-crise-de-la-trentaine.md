## Introduction

In French, there's an expression for the years just after your 20s: **la trentaine** — literally, "the thirties," or more precisely the stretch of years around turning 30. And there's a well-known companion phrase: **la crise de la trentaine**, the "thirties crisis." It's not officially recognized psychology, more a cultural shorthand people reach for casually — but having lived through my own version of it, I think I understand why it clusters so consistently around this particular age, and it has a lot to do with how the French education system structures the years before it.

## Milestones Until 24, Then Nothing

In France, most students follow a fairly standard path: the **bac** (baccalauréat) around age 18 is the most common diploma, often followed by further studies — a **classe préparatoire** and a **grande école**, roughly equivalent to a demanding university track, culminating in a **bac+5** (a master's-level degree, five years of study after the bac). Add an optional **année de césure** (gap year, often spent doing internships), and many French graduates start their first full-time job around 24.

Until that point, life has a rhythm built entirely around milestones. At six, you know you'll start *collège* (middle school) at eleven. At eleven, you know *lycée* (high school) is coming at fifteen. At every stage, there's an exam, a semester, a diploma — a clear deadline pulling you forward. Then you start working, and for the first time, that structure disappears completely. Nobody tells you what comes next. Nobody sets your deadlines for you anymore.

## Why Time Suddenly Moves Faster

This shift has two effects. First, time seems to accelerate. During school, you're constantly aware of time passing because there's always a next milestone marking it. Once you start working, without that external structure, years blur together far more easily.

Second — and this is where I think the real "crisis" begins — you start questioning things you never had time to question before. In my case, I worked as a data analyst for five years: three at one company, then two at another after a fairly standard move (a common pattern in my field: gain experience for a few years, switch companies, negotiate a raise, and get a fresh, engaging environment again). What I noticed both times was the same trajectory: everything feels stimulating and new for roughly two to three years, and then, once you've fully mastered your role, boredom sets in. Without external pressure or challenge, I found I simply stopped pushing myself further — I did exactly what my job description required, and no more.

## Reassessing What "Success" Means

Around 29, after that second round of boredom, I started genuinely questioning whether the career path I'd built — the one that looked objectively "good" on paper, with a solid salary and a respectable title — was actually what I wanted for the rest of my working life. During my studies, I'd absorbed a fairly conventional idea of success: a prestigious job, a strong salary, a certain professional status. Once I was actually living inside that success, those parameters started to matter less to me than I expected.

My wife and I ended up taking a full sabbatical year in Japan, right around both our 30th birthdays — which, I'll admit, is a fairly clichéd way to have a "crisis of the thirties." But the year did exactly what a change of environment is supposed to do: we met people with completely different life paths — WWOOFing on a farm, meeting career changes and unconventional choices we'd never have encountered in our previous, fairly homogeneous professional circle — and none of them seemed any less happy for having taken a different route. That alone was clarifying: there isn't only one valid way to build a life.

## What I Actually Did About It

When we came back to France, I became a French teacher and started creating educational content — which is, quite literally, the reason this article exists. I now earn noticeably less than I did as a data analyst, and being a French teacher carries a lower social status in France than the vague, impressive-sounding job title I used to have (a title that, honestly, meant something to almost no one who heard it). But I enjoy what I do now far more, and that trade-off has felt worth it so far.

## Is It Universal?

I don't know whether "la crise de la trentaine" is a distinctly French phenomenon or something closer to universal — quarter-life and mid-career crises get discussed in plenty of other cultures too. What does seem specific to the French context is how sharply the education system's constant milestones contrast with the sudden lack of structure that follows it, which may explain why this particular age — right after the deadlines stop — is when so many French people start reassessing everything at once.

## Conclusion

If you're in your late 20s or early 30s and suddenly questioning a path that used to feel obvious, you're not doing anything wrong — you're experiencing what French culture has a name for, and a fairly predictable shape for. The years of structured milestones end, time starts moving strangely fast, and for the first time, no one but you is deciding what comes next. That can be destabilizing. It can also, as it did for me, be the beginning of something better.
