## Introduction

As a teenager getting into American culture — rock music, skateboarding, surfing — I kept coming across the phrase "pardon my French," used right before someone said something rude or vulgar. For years I didn't really understand the joke. It wasn't until I started teaching French professionally that it clicked: a striking number of extremely common, everyday French expressions are, translated literally, genuinely vulgar. This article explains a few of the most common ones — not to encourage you to use them casually, but because you'll hear them constantly in native content, and understanding them is part of understanding real spoken French.

A quick note before we start: these expressions are casual/familiar register. They're common in everyday conversation, YouTube videos, and shows like *Call My Agent!* (*Dix pour cent* in French), but you won't find them in written journalism or formal contexts.

## "Putain"

Literally, *putain* means "prostitute," used in a very negative, insulting sense historically. In practice, French people use it constantly as an all-purpose exclamation — functioning almost exactly like "fuck" in English. It can express anger ("*putain, la voiture a un problème*" — "damn, the car's broken"), or excitement and surprise ("*putain, il a marqué !*" — "no way, he scored!"). The meaning depends entirely on tone and context, not on the word itself.

## "C'est le bordel"

Literally, a *bordel* is a brothel. But "*c'est le bordel*" is an extremely common way to say "it's a mess" — used constantly, including by parents telling their kids to tidy their room: "*c'est le bordel dans ta chambre.*" It also works for chaotic situations generally: "*il y avait des problèmes de train, c'était vraiment le bordel*" ("there were train problems, it was total chaos").

## "Casse-couilles"

This one comes from *casser* (to break) plus a vulgar word for male genitals — roughly equivalent to "it's a pain in the ass" or "it pisses me off" in English. Despite the literal anatomy, both men and women use this expression freely. "*La conjugaison en français, c'est un peu casse-couille*" is a perfectly normal thing to say, even in front of family — it's vulgar, but not shocking in the way a direct insult would be.

## "Ça me fait chier" / "C'est chiant"

Literally, this translates to something like "it makes me poop" — *chier* being a crude verb for that bodily function. In practice, it simply means "it's annoying" or "that pisses me off." You'll hear a French driver mutter "*c'est chiant, il y a des embouteillages*" (traffic is so annoying) without any conscious connection to the literal meaning at all — the vulgarity has essentially been bleached out through everyday use.

## "C'est chiant à mourir"

Building on the same root, this means "it's incredibly boring" (literally something closer to "it makes me poop to death"). You could say "*on est allé voir le dernier film et c'était chiant à mourir*" to friends, or even to your own parents — mildly vulgar, but well within the range of totally normal daily speech.

## "Je suis dans la merde"

Literally "I'm in the shit," this means "I'm in trouble" or "I have a real problem." It's used constantly and casually: "*j'ai pas fait ma déclaration d'impôts, je suis dans la merde*" (I didn't file my taxes, I'm in trouble). The equivalent English phrase carries roughly the same weight and the same casual usage.

## "C'est de la merde"

Similarly, this describes something of poor quality: "*j'ai acheté ça, c'est vraiment pas de la bonne qualité, c'est un peu de la merde*" (I bought this, the quality isn't great, it's kind of crap).

## A Superstition Worth Knowing: Don't Say "Bonne Chance"

Here's a genuinely useful piece of French culture: before an exam, a job interview, a driving test, or anything similarly high-stakes, wishing someone "*bonne chance*" (good luck) is considered bad luck. Instead, the traditional thing to say is simply "*merde*" — the same crude word discussed above, used here almost like the English theatre tradition of "break a leg." You'll hear parents tell their kids "*merde pour le bac*" (good luck on your exam) rather than the literal "good luck." It's not a hard rule — nothing terrible happens if you say "bonne chance" instead — but if you want to sound genuinely French, this is the phrase to use.

## Why This Matters for Learners

None of this vocabulary shows up in textbooks or structured podcasts made for learners, and for good reason — it's not something you need to actively produce, especially early on. But if you start watching native French content, a French Netflix show, or a YouTuber you enjoy, there's a strong chance a good portion of these expressions will show up regularly. Recognizing them — rather than being thrown off by the literal, often absurd meaning — is a real and useful step toward understanding French as it's actually spoken, not just as it's taught.

## Conclusion

The gap between the "correct," textbook version of French and how people actually talk day to day is often exactly this: casual, mildly vulgar expressions that have long since lost their literal shock value through sheer repetition. You don't need to start swearing in French to sound fluent. But understanding why a French person might casually say something that translates to "it makes me poop" when they're simply annoyed about traffic — that's a genuinely useful piece of the language, and a good answer to why English speakers have been saying "pardon my French" for so long.
