## Introduction

"If I really want to learn French, I need to move to France." It's one of the most common assumptions in language learning — and, having spent a full year living in Japan specifically to improve my Japanese, I can say fairly confidently: it's mostly false. Simply being physically present in a country doesn't make you fluent. What you do before you arrive matters far more than most people expect.

## The Canadian Couple Who Spoke No Japanese After Four Years

When my wife and I moved to Japan for a sabbatical year, we started taking Japanese lessons in the small town where we lived, from local retirees who taught informally. In these classes, we met a Canadian couple who had already been living in Japan for four years — and were only just beginning to learn Japanese.

This isn't a criticism of them personally; it's a demonstration of a real pattern. The reason it happened is straightforward: they arrived speaking only English, and their entire life quickly organized itself around English. Their jobs were in English, their colleagues spoke English, and even everyday errands like grocery shopping could mostly be handled with basic English, since enough people understand a little of it, even in Japan. Living in the country simply didn't force the issue.

We saw a version of the same thing ourselves. Living with Japanese roommates, we tried speaking Japanese with them, but our level wasn't strong enough to sustain a real conversation without it becoming awkward or difficult for everyone involved. Put yourself in the other person's position: if a foreigner in France struggles to speak French with you, you'll help out a little — but for genuine daily conversation, it's simply more efficient to switch to whatever language is easiest. That happens just as easily in Paris as it does in Tokyo.

## The First Reason: Theory Doesn't Require Geography

The first flaw in "I'll learn once I'm there" is that a huge portion of what you need to learn has nothing to do with physical location. Japanese kanji — the roughly 2,000 characters needed for basic literacy — take the same amount of time and effort to learn whether you study them in Tokyo or in your childhood bedroom back home. Being physically present in Japan doesn't make character memorization go any faster.

The same logic applies to French conjugation. It's genuinely tedious, but it's also something you can learn identically anywhere — there's no advantage to waiting until you're in France to sit down and learn the passé composé or the subjunctive. If a language is significantly different from your own, the theoretical groundwork — grammar rules, verb systems, an unfamiliar alphabet — is worth tackling before you arrive, precisely because none of it benefits from proximity. Waiting until you're on the ground just delays progress you could have made months earlier.

## The Second Reason: Without a Baseline, Immersion Doesn't Work

If you arrive with zero prior knowledge, being physically surrounded by the language isn't automatically an advantage — it can just as easily become a wall of noise you can't parse at all. If you don't know any of the Japanese writing systems, walking through Tokyo's streets covered in Japanese text doesn't teach you to read; it's simply unreadable. Similarly, arriving in rural France without basic French conjugation means every conversation immediately hits a barrier, regardless of how much French is happening around you.

Interestingly, the reverse case shows how much easier this is between closely related languages. If I moved to Spain tomorrow, I could reasonably start learning directly on the ground, because French and Spanish share enough vocabulary and structure that a French speaker has a real head start — swap the ending of a word and you're often already close to correct. The more different two languages are, the more essential it becomes to build a foundation before arriving, rather than relying on osmosis once you're there.

## The Third Reason: Learning Still Takes the Time It Takes

Even with the best possible method, there's a limit to how much you can accelerate the process — call it an "incompressible" amount of learning. Two thousand kanji, for example, simply take the time they take to memorize, wherever you are. Your brain needs to digest information gradually, the same way your body needs time to digest food — send it too much at once and it can't process it efficiently, no matter how motivated you are.

This isn't a reason to be discouraged; it's a reason to start earlier. If the learning is going to take a certain amount of time regardless of location, doing that work before you move means you arrive with a head start rather than a deficit.

## What Arriving at an Intermediate Level Actually Unlocks

The real argument for building a foundation before you go isn't just efficiency — it's the experiences an intermediate level makes possible. During our year in Japan, I'd estimate that roughly 70% of the most memorable moments we had happened specifically because we could speak a bit of Japanese — nothing close to fluent, just functional.

One example: through a roommate's friend, we met a Japanese family who spoke no English at all. They invited us to spend several days at their home in the mountains, entirely in Japanese, hesitant and imperfect as our Japanese was. We went skiing with them. None of that would have been possible with English alone — not because the family was unfriendly, but because there simply would have been no way to communicate enough to build that connection in the first place.

The same logic applies to France. If you head out to rural France with even a basic level of French, you'll likely be welcomed warmly and given a very different experience than a purely English-speaking visit would offer. In big cities, this kind of spontaneous connection is rarer everywhere — Paris has the same reputation for brusqueness that Tokyo does, mostly because everyone's in a hurry and there are simply more foreigners around, diluting any single interaction's uniqueness. It's outside the big cities, in smaller towns, where a bit of the local language tends to open the most doors.

## Conclusion

If you're waiting to "really learn" French until you move there, you're likely delaying progress you could be making right now, on grammar and vocabulary that will take exactly as long to learn wherever you study it. Living in France is genuinely valuable — but it amplifies whatever level you arrive with rather than creating one from nothing. Build a real intermediate foundation first, and the country will do the rest; arrive with nothing, and you risk spending years, like that Canadian couple, living in France without ever really living in French.
