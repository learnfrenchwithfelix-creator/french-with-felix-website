## Introduction

Most advice about language learning warns you about frustration and demotivation — and rightly so, since giving up is the worst outcome for any learner. But there's a less obvious danger that rarely gets mentioned: being *too* motivated. It sounds like a strange thing to warn against, but after years of watching it happen to myself with Japanese, I think it deserves as much attention as its opposite.

## The Bookstore Trap

There's a Japanese bookshop in Paris, near Opéra, that I used to visit fairly often while learning Japanese on my own. Every time I walked in, I felt a genuine surge of motivation — I'd picture myself buying a grammar book or a novel, going home, and working through it seriously. I bought several books this way over the years.

Almost none of them ever got opened again after that first day. The pattern was consistent: a sharp spike of motivation in the store, followed by a crash once I got home and reality set back in. And the stronger the spike, the harder the crash — those unopened books sitting on my shelf became a source of guilt rather than progress, a quiet reminder that I'd spent money on something I never used.

The same thing happened with manga. I once bought a full set of Japanese Boruto volumes, imagining I'd enjoy the story while building vocabulary at the same time. I got home, opened the first volume, and immediately hit a wall — I didn't know enough words to follow even a simple page. I gave up after a few pages, and those volumes sat unread for a long time until, eventually, with more vocabulary built up elsewhere, I could actually read manga in Japanese and enjoy it. The timing, not the motivation, was what mattered.

## Where This Surface-Level Motivation Comes From

A few patterns tend to generate this kind of spike-and-crash motivation:

**"Fluent in three months" content.** YouTube — and I say this as someone who makes YouTube content — rewards titles that promise fast, dramatic results. "Become fluent in three months" gets clicks in a way that "this will take years of steady work" never will. These videos aren't necessarily wrong about the methods they present, but the framing creates false urgency and unrealistic timelines that set learners up for disappointment.

**The illusion of progress through spending.** Buying a grammar book, downloading an app, or paying for a course can feel like progress in the moment, simply because you've taken action. But none of it actually teaches you anything until you use it. This applies just as much to content creation, incidentally — watching a video titled "3 tricks to grow your channel" gives the same false sense of having done something productive.

**Setting dramatic goals as a substitute for working toward them.** I did this myself when I announced I'd learn Portuguese well enough to speak with my family in three months. Announcing a big goal out loud creates a strange, internal sense of having already started — even when no actual work has happened yet. The goal itself becomes a stand-in for the effort it's supposed to represent.

## Why This Turns Into Frustration

The mechanism is simple: motivation that isn't grounded in actual work is imagined, not earned. It's built on a mental picture of future success rather than evidence of present progress. And imagined motivation, precisely because it isn't tied to anything real, evaporates the moment reality intrudes — the unopened book, the missed goal, the native content you still can't follow after your "three months to fluency."

The real danger isn't the spike itself — it's what follows. If you set a goal like "fluent in six months" and, six months later, you've clearly improved but you're nowhere near fluent, the natural reaction for many people is to conclude the whole effort failed, rather than recognizing genuine progress that simply didn't match an unrealistic bar.

## What Sustainable Motivation Looks Like Instead

The alternative isn't to avoid motivation — it's to source it from a different place: your own accumulated work, rather than imagined future success.

- **Use statistics from your actual practice.** A study streak, a vocabulary count, a heatmap of days studied — these are motivating precisely because they're built from real, completed work, not projected results.
- **Let goals act as a compass, not a countdown.** A target like an official exam gives you direction and a reason to broaden your practice, but it shouldn't be the source of your daily motivation — the habit itself should be.
- **Notice how far you've come, not just how far you have to go.** If you can understand a slow-paced podcast today that you couldn't follow six months ago, that's real, earned motivation — quieter than a bookstore spike, but far more durable.
- **Enjoy the process itself.** Ultimately, the deepest, most stable source of motivation isn't a goal at all — it's genuinely enjoying the act of listening, reading, and practicing daily, independent of any external outcome.

## Conclusion

It's not that ambition or excitement are bad — they're often exactly what gets someone started. The danger is mistaking a motivation spike for actual progress, and building goals or purchases around a feeling that's guaranteed to fade. Real, sustainable motivation comes from the habit itself and from what you've already built, not from what you imagine you're about to build. Stay excited about French — just make sure that excitement is pointed at the daily practice, not at the next book you're about to buy and never open.
