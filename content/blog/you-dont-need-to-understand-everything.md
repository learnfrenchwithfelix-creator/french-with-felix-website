## Introduction

When people ask me how to learn a language, my answer is almost always the same: do as much immersion as possible. Listen, watch, read — build automatic reflexes through repeated exposure. But I've noticed a pattern with almost every learner I recommend this to: the moment they hit a word they don't understand, they pause. "Sorry, I don't get this, I'm not going to make it." And that instinct, more than anything else, is what makes immersion feel ineffective.

## Immersion Isn't About Perfect Comprehension

Think about how you read in your own native language. You occasionally hit a word you couldn't define if someone asked you to — and you keep reading anyway, because you can guess its meaning from context. That skill, guessing meaning from surrounding information, is exactly what immersion is meant to train. If you stop every time you don't understand something, you never build it.

The same principle applies to speaking. Students who overthink every sentence before saying it end up sounding hesitant and unsure. It's often more useful to say something imperfect quickly than to search for the "correct" version silently. I do this myself in Portuguese: I write imperfect sentences to an AI chatbot and let it correct me afterward, rather than trying to get everything right the first time.

## Reading With What You Already Know

When I started learning Portuguese, I picked books I'd already read in French — the first Harry Potter, then *Memoirs of a Geisha*. That choice matters more than it sounds. Because I already knew the plot, I could follow the story even on pages where I barely understood a sentence. I didn't need every word; I just needed to recognize *where* I was in a story I already knew. That's a very different, much lower-pressure kind of reading than trying to parse an unfamiliar text word by word.

## Feeding Your Brain, Not Controlling It

Here's a useful mental model: immersion is like feeding your brain. The more input you give it, the more it has to work with — but *how* it processes and uses that information isn't something you control directly. There's actually research on the natural order in which learners acquire grammar and vocabulary, regardless of the order they're taught in. In other words, you can't force your brain to learn a specific rule just because you want to use it right now.

That means the goal of immersion isn't to master a specific sentence or memorize every rule you encounter. It's to expose yourself to as much authentic French as possible so your brain can absorb patterns in its own order, at its own pace. A minimal level of comprehension is necessary — if you understand literally nothing, there's no input to digest — but perfect comprehension isn't the goal, and demanding it of yourself will only slow you down.

## A Detective's Mindset

When you listen to native French, you won't have the option to pause and look something up mid-conversation. What you need instead is the ability to catch the overall shape of what's being said — a bit like detective work. One example I give my students: in spoken French, we almost never pronounce the "ne" in negative sentences (*je ne sais pas* becomes *je sais pas*). If you're listening for "ne" as your signal for negation, you'll miss it constantly. The real signal is "pas." Training your ear to catch these small, isolated cues — rather than demanding full grammatical precision — is what lets you follow fast, natural speech.

## A Practical Flashcard Rule

If you do want to build vocabulary from something you're reading, here's the rule I follow: one flashcard per sentence, with no more than one or two unknown words in it. Trying to memorize every unfamiliar word in a difficult sentence at once isn't efficient — it overloads the card and makes it harder to retain. Better to let the words that keep reappearing naturally sink in through repeated exposure, and reserve flashcards for the ones that matter most.

## Conclusion

Immersion works because of repeated, imperfect exposure — not because of total comprehension. If you're pausing every video the moment you miss a word, you're training the wrong skill. Let some things go, follow the global meaning, and trust that your brain will digest the rest of the information in its own time. The goal isn't to understand 100% of what you hear today — it's to understand a little more each time you try.
