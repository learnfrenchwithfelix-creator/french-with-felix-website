## Introduction

I've traveled since I was a child, mostly thanks to my parents — France gives students two months of summer vacation, and mine often used it for home exchanges, swapping our house with families abroad for three or four weeks at a time. It was an economical, slightly unusual way to see the world. But there's a real difference between traveling as a child, everything organized and secure around you, and traveling as an adult with no fixed plan, unsure what's going to happen next. That second kind of travel is what my wife and I experienced during our year-long working holiday in Japan — and it taught me something about what actually makes a trip memorable.

## The Plan That Didn't Survive Contact With Reality

We arrived with a loose outline: travel the length of the country from south to north, catch the cherry blossoms in bloom, see as many regions, landscapes, and traditions as possible. Almost none of it happened as planned — and that's exactly where the real experience began.

We landed in Tokyo with ten days booked in an Airbnb, enough time to find a room in a shared house. We found one within a couple of days, moving in with a mix of Japanese and international housemates. Barely two or three days in, one of our Japanese roommates asked what we planned to do next. We explained we wanted to start our trip in Kagoshima, in the far south, to catch the cherry blossoms. Her response caught us completely off guard: "Oh, you could just stay at my grandmother's place."

## An Unexpected Kind of Hospitality

She'd known us for less than 48 hours. Given the reputation Japanese people have for being reserved and private, we were genuinely a little suspicious at first — but we decided to accept. That decision is where the actual adventure started.

We ended up in a traditional Japanese house in the countryside — tatami mats, sliding doors, the exact atmosphere of a Studio Ghibli film. The grandmother spoke no English; we spoke only a handful of words of Japanese. And yet we shared real, meaningful moments together. It taught us something we kept relearning throughout the trip: the best experiences are rarely the ones you plan.

## When Planning Works Against You

After that week in Kagoshima, we continued as originally scheduled — moving north by train, hotels booked ahead of time. That portion of the trip, while genuinely beautiful, left a much fainter impression. It felt more touristic: faster, more impersonal. We visited guidebook-recommended sites, dropped off our bags, took photos, and moved on. We rarely met anyone.

That contrast is what pushed us to change how we traveled entirely. We stopped planning ahead and started following opportunities as they came — chance encounters, spontaneous invitations, whatever felt interesting in the moment. WWOOFing (volunteer farm work in exchange for room and board) became one of our main tools for this. Our first WWOOFing stop, in Takayama, put us in touch with a young Taiwanese traveler who recommended another place entirely — so we went there instead. Each stage of the trip got decided through conversation, word of mouth, one connection opening onto the next.

## Small Moments, Real Connections

One day in Tokyo, I mentioned to a housemate that I needed a haircut. He told me his mother was a hairdresser, in Nagoya. Weeks later, passing through between other stops, we stopped by her place. She took us out to dinner, and we spent a genuinely wonderful evening together. She's visiting us in Paris this summer. None of that would have happened if we'd kept following a fixed itinerary.

Looking back, it was these small, unplanned encounters — not the temples, the landscapes, or the museums, however beautiful they were — that left the deepest impression. Traveling this way takes time; it's not something you can really do in a rushed two-week trip. It requires being willing to get a little lost, to let a plan change, to hand over some control to whatever the day brings.

## Coming Home Changed

What surprised me most wasn't anything about Japan itself, but what the trip did to how I saw France afterward. Coming back, I found myself rediscovering things I'd always taken for granted — bread, cheese, the architecture, the small neighborhood cafés — and suddenly seeing them differently. It's as if the trip taught me to appreciate my own country more, precisely by giving me some distance from it. The first time I walked back through Paris after returning, I noticed, almost for the first time, how genuinely beautiful the city is — something I'd become a little numb to before leaving.

## Conclusion

Travel, at its best, isn't really about the places on your itinerary — it's the surprises, the unplanned encounters, the moments you could never have scheduled in advance. Those are the ones that end up becoming your most vivid memories, and, oddly enough, they're also often the ones that teach you the most about the place you left behind. If you get the chance to travel with real time on your hands, consider leaving space in the plan for exactly that kind of unpredictability — it might end up being the whole point of the trip.
